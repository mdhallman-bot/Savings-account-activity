Simple Interest vs. Compound Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, every single period — the amount of interest earned never changes, even as time passes. This shows up most often in certain loans (some auto loans, for example) and some bonds.

Compound interest is calculated on the principal PLUS any interest already earned — so the amount of interest earned grows every period. This is how virtually every deposit account works: all four account types you just compared — regular savings, online savings, money market, and CDs — pay compound interest, usually advertised as an APY (Annual Percentage Yield).

Here's the gap in action — $500 at 5% for 3 years, calculated both ways:

YearSimple Interest TotalCompound Interest Total
1$525.00$525.00
2$550.00$551.25
3$575.00$578.81

Same rate, same time, same starting amount — but compound interest pulls ahead every year after the first, because it's earning interest on interest.

Project Jasmine's Growth — Step by Step

Suppose Jasmine puts her $1,000 into an Online Savings account earning 4% APY, compounded annually, and leaves it completely untouched for 3 years. Don't just look up a final number — work through it one year at a time. Each year, interest is earned on that year's starting balance (which includes all interest earned so far).

How to Calculate It — Worked Example

Each year, repeat these 3 steps:

  1. Multiply that year's starting balance by the interest rate. This gives you that year's interest.
  2. Add the interest to the starting balance. This gives you the new ending balance.
  3. Next year, that ending balance becomes the new starting balance — go back to step 1.

Example (different numbers than Jasmine's, so you can't just copy it): $200 at 10% for 2 years.

YearStarting BalanceStep 1: InterestStep 2: Ending Balance
1$200.00$200.00 × 0.10 = $20.00$200.00 + $20.00 = $220.00
2$220.00 (last year's ending balance)$220.00 × 0.10 = $22.00$220.00 + $22.00 = $242.00

Notice Year 2's interest ($22.00) is bigger than Year 1's ($20.00) — because it was calculated on $220, not the original $200. That's the whole idea of compound interest in three steps. Now use this exact method on Jasmine's numbers below: $1,000 to start, 4% each year.
