Understanding Compound Interest

To actually earn compound interest, your money needs to sit in an account that compounds — meaning interest gets calculated on your whole growing balance, not just what you originally put in. All four account types you just ranked do this.

Quick reality check: almost every savings account you'd actually open pays compound interest. Simple interest is mostly a teaching tool for understanding the math — it's rarely a real choice you'll face at the bank. The real decision you'll make is about the rate of return and how locked-away your money is, not simple vs. compound. We'll dig much deeper into rates of return in the Investing unit.

For money you won't touch for many years, some people also use retirement accounts (like a 401(k) or IRA) or diversified investments like index funds. These aren't FDIC insured and carry more risk than a savings account, but they've historically offered higher average returns over long stretches of time — which is exactly why starting early matters so much.
